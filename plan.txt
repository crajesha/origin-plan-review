Robotic Drywall Finishing

First-Round Experimental Validation Plan | Apprentice — Experimentation
& Systems Engineering

Candidate: Chigateri Rajesha Date: 12 August 2026

Problem framing. Repeated robot finishing trials produce inconsistent
tapered-seam quality: underfill in the taper valley, excess material
buildup, visible edge lines, and post-sand surface waviness. The brief
does not identify a root cause. My objective is therefore to separate
process variation from measurement variation and identify which
subsystem(s) deserve the next round of controlled testing.

1. System understanding

[]

Figure 1. System-level diagram — motion, end-effector, material,
sensing, and geometry feed the finishing process, with environment and
measurement method acting across the whole system.

Key systems judgment: I would not start by blaming material. The same
visible defect can be produced by different mechanisms — for example,
underfill could reflect insufficient deposition, excessive speed,
excessive standoff, or a material-state change. The first round should
therefore test one representative factor per subsystem under matched
conditions, while tracking measurement reliability and trial order.

Drywall quality target. A correctly finished tapered seam should
transition smoothly into the surrounding board so the joint is not
visually or physically prominent after finishing/sanding. Underfill
leaves the recess insufficiently filled; buildup leaves excess compound;
edge lines indicate an abrupt/visible transition; waviness indicates
residual surface non-flatness after sanding. These are quality
departures, not merely sensor signals.

Primary working hypothesis: the observed variation is likely
multi-factor rather than a single-cause failure. My first-round goal is
to determine whether the largest repeatable changes trace to motion,
tool behavior, sensing/standoff, material state, or their interactions —
not to declare a root cause from one trial.

2. Experiment design

Objective: quantify baseline repeatability, then screen representative
controllable factors from the four major subsystems while holding
geometry, measurement setup, and the remaining process settings fixed.

Working hypotheses

-   H1 — Motion: path velocity and tool orientation can change
    deposition/finishing consistency and therefore seam profile,
    underfill/buildup, or edge-line severity.

-   H2 — End-effector: application-tool condition/rate and sanding
    contact force can change material deposition and final surface
    waviness.

-   H3 — Sensing: standoff error or sensor calibration drift can create
    real process changes and/or apparent quality changes; measurement
    reliability must be validated before attributing process variation.

-   H4 — Material: viscosity/mix-state variation can change how
    consistently compound is deposited and finished.

Important: these are testable hypotheses, not conclusions. Evidence for
one factor should be interpreted only after its competing explanations
are controlled.

2.2 Controls, disturbances and confounders

  -------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Category        Examples / control action
  --------------- ---------------------------------------------------------
  Controlled      Same seam geometry/panel type; same tool path except
                  tested factor; same compound preparation protocol; same
                  sanding procedure; same measurement fixture and
                  camera/sensor pose.

  Disturbances    Temperature/humidity; material aging during the session;
                  tool wear; sensor thermal drift; panel-to-panel
                  variation.

  Confounders     Changing velocity and standoff together; allowing
                  viscosity to drift with time; tool wear correlated with
                  trial number; recalibrating only after poor runs;
                  lighting changes between measurements.

  Randomization / Randomize factor-level order where practical; interleave
  blocking        baseline checks so drift is visible; record trial
                  timestamp and tool/material state.
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------

Why this structure? A baseline block tells me how noisy the process is
before I attribute differences to a factor. Interleaved baseline checks
help expose drift. The four subsystem blocks provide a practical first
screen without pretending a 20-run study can estimate every interaction.

3.1 Measurement-system reliability

If imaging/ranging is used, the measurement system becomes another
subsystem in the experiment. I would use a fixed camera/sensor mount,
controlled lighting, a calibration artifact/reference surface before the
session, and periodic calibration checks. Standoff should be recorded
because a change in sensor-to-surface distance can look like a process
change. For force-torque data, record zero/bias checks and watch for
drift over time.

Fair comparison rule: if a calibration check fails, flag the affected
run rather than silently correcting the result after seeing the outcome.
Re-run the measurement under a known-good setup where possible. This
prevents measurement correction from becoming outcome-dependent.

3.2 Primary response variable

I would keep several physical metrics rather than collapsing everything
immediately into one score. A composite quality score can hide
trade-offs — for example, a seam with good flatness but visible edge
lines. For screening, I would rank factors by consistent effect across
the key metrics and by practical rework impact.

4. Analysis and decision-making

Step 1 — Establish baseline variation. Calculate run-to-run spread under
nominal settings. This is the reference against which factor changes are
judged.

Step 2 — Compare within-factor contrasts. For each subsystem block,
compare the two tested levels while keeping the other variables fixed.
Examine the direction and size of the change across repeats, not a
single best/worst run.

Step 3 — Check drift and confounding. Plot results against trial
order/time. If quality deteriorates monotonically, tool wear, material
aging, or sensor drift may be responsible rather than the tested factor.

Step 4 — Separate observation from conclusion. Example: “Higher velocity
coincided with more underfill in both repeats” is an observation.
“Velocity is the root cause” is not justified until material state,
standoff, tool condition, and measurement reliability are controlled.

Step 5 — Decide the next test from uncertainty. If one factor shows a
repeatable effect, confirm it with more repeats and tighter controls. If
two factors appear coupled, test their interaction. If results are
noisy, first validate the measurement/process baseline rather than
adding more factors.

7. Second-round prioritization — if only 15–20 runs are available

I would not pre-commit the second round to a subsystem before seeing
first-round evidence. Instead, I would rank candidates using three
questions: (1) is the effect larger than baseline variation? (2) is it
repeatable across trials and not explained by drift/confounding? (3)
does it have a plausible physical mechanism and practical quality
impact?

7.1 Example decision logic

-   If motion shows the clearest repeatable effect: confirm
    velocity/orientation with more repeats, hold material viscosity and
    standoff tightly, then test whether the effect remains after a
    controlled tool-condition check.

-   If material shows the clearest effect: characterize material state
    first and repeat at controlled viscosity/rest conditions before
    changing robot motion. This avoids blaming the robot for a feedstock
    problem.

-   If sensing/measurement is unstable: do not optimize the process yet.
    First establish a trustworthy measurement baseline; otherwise the
    experiment may optimize sensor artifacts.

-   If two factors remain comparable: reserve runs for their
    interaction. A factor that looks weak alone may matter when paired
    with another subsystem state.

8. Engineering conclusion

My current read is deliberately provisional: the problem should be
treated as a cross-subsystem variation problem. The highest-value first
step is not selecting a root cause; it is establishing a reliable
baseline and then screening one representative, controllable factor from
motion, end-effector, sensing/standoff, and material under matched
conditions. The dominant driver is the factor whose effect is
repeatable, larger than baseline/measurement variation, physically
plausible, and still present after confounders are controlled.

This approach directly addresses the brief's emphasis on systems
thinking, experimentation rigor, measurement discipline,
analysis/judgment, and understanding of tapered-seam finish quality.
Where the brief does not provide numerical setpoints or sensor
specifications, I have stated assumptions rather than inventing them.
